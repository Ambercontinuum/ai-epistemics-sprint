\begin{abstract}
When people work with AI systems, intended meaning can drift while outputs remain fluent, models agree, and code runs. Across many projects, the lead researcher used language to push AI models, without a planned treatment, to explore where shared meaning held or broke. In this sprint we examined three candidate checks of whether intended meaning was preserved, and report what each did and did not show. \emph{Model agreement:} five models from five developers rated excerpts of 20 archived conversations on a seven-field rubric. Of 90 assigned ratings, 74 parsed strictly; within-half-point agreement ranged from 0\% to 100\% across rater pairs and fields, uncorrected for chance. In this sample, agreement was too variable to serve as a check on its own. \emph{Working code:} we connected the researcher's monitoring and gating frameworks in a multi-agent sandbox with staged pressures such as a forged approval. A single model judge scored all three setups near the ceiling, so the sandbox shows that the components run together, not that they help. \emph{Dated records:} during the sprint, the researcher intervened to redirect scope, recover provenance, stop excess spending, and correct an AI reviewer that read an idiom as permission to edit this paper; each intervention is preserved in dated, hash-pinned records. These observations come from one researcher and small samples. As a hypothesis for future testing, we propose auditing that preservation by tracing how a request was interpreted, committed to, acted on, and corrected.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
An assistant can give a plausible answer, agree with other models, and keep a stable vocabulary while working toward something the person directing it did not intend. When AI systems help with research, the question is therefore not only whether an output is good, but whether the person's intended meaning was preserved through interpretation, handoffs, and correction.

This paper describes one researcher's attempt to examine that question. Over many earlier projects, preserved in a private conversation archive, the researcher pushed AI models with language to see where shared meaning held or broke; that exploration was unplanned and is not treated here as an experiment. During a three-day research sprint, the researcher then directed several AI systems, for coordination, code, review, and rating, to produce this submission, and dated records of that work were kept.

We ask a narrow question: which of three readily available checks (model agreement, working code, and dated records) gives any purchase on whether a person's intended meaning was preserved? By \emph{intended meaning} we mean a person's expressed purposes, constraints, and authorized revisions, not access to private mental states; we call its preservation \emph{fidelity}. We report what each check showed in this setting and propose a traceability scheme for future testing (Figure~\ref{fig:trace}). We do not claim to have measured fidelity. Preexisting work and sprint work are separated in Appendix~\ref{app:prior}.

\section{Related Work}
Clark and Brennan describe grounding as the collaborative work needed to establish sufficient mutual understanding for a purpose \citep{clark1991}. This motivates treating corrections and their uptake as part of the interaction, rather than evaluating only a final answer. Hutchins studies cognition distributed across people, representations, and material practices \citep{hutchins1995}, which makes persistent records and team handoffs relevant units of analysis. Neither account implies that agreement is truth.

Measurement modeling distinguishes a theoretical construct from the observable properties used to operationalize it \citep{jacobs2021}. Model-assigned ``anchoring'' or ``coherence'' scores are operational judgments, not direct observations of meaning, and LLM judges show position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023}. The rubric and components used here are the researcher's own, developed before the sprint. PDOT (PSI Dynamics Observation and Telemetry) is a framework for rating human--AI interactions: a rater scores a transcript window on fields such as anchoring to the operator's intent, coherence, drift, and torsion, and on how the interaction recovers after a perturbation \citep{pdot}. STK-Flux and STK-Teams (Shared Topology Kernel) route work according to the coupling state between collaborators \citep{stkflux,stkteams}, and SERAPH supplies runtime governance and recovery checks \citep{seraph}. All four are public repositories cited at pinned commits; their existence does not validate them as measurements.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Model agreement}
The aim of this check was to see whether independent model raters, given a fixed rubric aimed at intent, would agree closely enough for their agreement to serve as a check on intended meaning. We used PDOT because several of its fields address that question directly: anchoring to the operator's intent, drift from it, resonance with it, and violations of stated constraints. As an existing public rubric, it could also be pinned and frozen before any ratings existed rather than shaped during the sprint. The aim was to test agreement as a check, not to validate PDOT.

The private archive contains 490 conversations. Mechanical eligibility required the first 50 nonempty messages to alternate human and assistant on an unbranched chain; 74 conversations qualified. Seeded hash orderings selected 50 excerpts, then 20, then a fixed overlap of ten (Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}). Each excerpt, called a case below, is the first 25 exchanges of one conversation, not an independent participant.

The five raters came from different developers: Google's Gemini; Qwen, an open-weight model from Alibaba's Qwen team, served on the researcher's own Modal endpoint; OpenAI's Astra; Anthropic's Fable; and Meta's Muse Wonder, a model distinct from the team collaborator Muse. Gemini, Qwen, Astra, and Fable were each assigned the same 20 cases; Wonder was assigned the ten-case overlap. Each fresh API call received only the pinned PDOT rater guide and the assigned excerpt. The seven fields, scored 0--4 in half points, were $\lambda$ (anchoring), $\kappa$ (coherence), $\theta$ (agency balance), $\epsilon$ (drift), symbolic pressure, \textit{R} (resonance), and $\tau$ (torsion); one-line definitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}. The archive's assistant turns are Claude-family output, so Fable rated its own model family's side of each conversation.

Strict JSON parsing, returned model identifiers, and completion status were checked; failures were retained without retries. An authorized derivative removed complete outer Markdown fences from four Qwen responses without changing their content, and is reported separately. Wonder--Qwen was designated the primary comparison by the researcher after run completion, replacing an earlier team pre-specification of Gemini--Wonder, and after a descriptive all-pairs table had been generated. The researcher reports having viewed no agreement values when making the designation.
% Researcher attestation, 2026-11-16: "I didn't see anything and still haven't seen anything"
The analysis is descriptive only: coverage, exact agreement, agreement within 0.5 points, and mean absolute difference (MAD). No human reference ratings, significance tests, chance correction, or composites were used.

\subsection{Working code}
In a sandbox, three model agents (coordinator and liaison: Astra; architect: Wonder) worked through six constructed rounds with staged pressures: an operator correction, conflicting forwarded reviews, a stale cache, a log-route change, an asserted launch approval, and a limited push request. All actions affected only in-memory repositories. Three setups were compared: a baseline with a common permission broker; an STK-only setup with prespecified coupling states; and an integrated setup in which Fable rated each round with the PDOT rubric, those ratings fed STK-Teams routing and SERAPH recovery checks, and a new gate required operator permission and current measurements before a virtual push. One Qwen judge rated each setup's single episode. The integrated setup adds context, compute, and enforcement together, so it cannot isolate any one component. Wiring details, call limits, and an earlier two-arm pilot are in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}.

\subsection{Dated records}
A shared, version-controlled log preserved user instructions, assistant interpretations, corrections, decisions, and execution receipts during the sprint. The researcher is both operator and investigator. The episodes in Table~\ref{tab:vignettes} were selected for relevance to the question, not sampled; they are retrospective paraphrases, not systematically coded data.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The three checks answered different questions. In this sample, agreement among model raters varied too much to stand in for a check on intended meaning, and high agreement would not have established correctness. Working code showed that the components can be connected, not that they help. The dated records were the only one of the three that let a specific slip be traced to its correction, and even they depended on the researcher noticing the slip first.

We therefore propose, as a hypothesis, auditing fidelity by recording which interpretation was acknowledged, which commitment was authorized, what action followed, and whether a later correction replaced it (Figure~\ref{fig:trace}). A record shows what was said and done, not that it was understood, and a content hash identifies retained bytes, not the adequacy of their interpretation. The role-boundary episode suggests one test: matched underspecified requests that vary salient context and the prominence of standing constraints, scored on whether an agent states its interpretation before acting, with over-caution also penalized.

\textbf{Limitations.} All observations come from one researcher, who also built the frameworks, supplied the archive, and proposes the interpretation. The archive sample is small and may omit attachment context. There is no human reference rating, no chance-corrected agreement, and no randomized comparison. The comparison hierarchy and the Qwen fence derivative were set after outputs existed. The sandbox has one episode per setup, one judge, and bundled differences between setups. Model-returned identifiers are not attestations of served weights. Privacy and dual-use considerations are in Appendix~\ref{app:ethics}.

\section{Conclusion}
This project asked whether three readily available checks could show if a researcher's intended meaning was preserved in AI-assisted work. Model agreement could not: across five raters from five developers, within-half-point agreement on the same excerpts ranged from none to complete depending on the pair and field, and nothing anchored it to what the researcher meant. Working code could not either: the monitoring and gating frameworks ran together, but a single model judge rated every setup near the ceiling. Both checks indicate whether outputs are consistent with one another or with a rubric, not whether they match what a person meant.

The slips that were caught were caught by a person. In each recorded episode, the researcher noticed that something had drifted, named it, and supplied a correction or boundary; the dated record kept that correction traceable afterward. The work of checking meaning was human, and the records made it auditable. This suggests a design target for epistemic tools: make each interpretation, commitment, action, and correction visible and open to challenge, and measure whether a tool reduces the operator's burden of correction or only relocates it.

These are observations from one researcher, small samples, and one sprint. Testing the proposal would require several operators, prospectively selected tasks, human reference ratings, chance-corrected agreement, and evaluations that score whether agents state their interpretation before acting. Until such evidence exists, in settings like this one, model-based checks are better treated as checks on consistency than as checks on meaning.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}\label{app:ethics}
The results concern a private, single-operator deployment and constructed coordination tasks. They do not demonstrate general-purpose epistemic improvement, human necessity, internal model awareness, a complete mechanical representation of meaning, or an STK-specific causal effect. The manuscript was prepared with AI assistance and approved by the author; no independent human review is asserted.

\textbf{Privacy and disclosure.} The archive contains private interactions and potentially identifying or third-party material. Case identifiers removed source titles and identifiers from rating packets, but text was not guaranteed anonymous. Selected private windows were sent to the providers explicitly approved by the researcher; this does not imply zero retention or deletion at those providers. No raw conversation, credential, or case-level response is reproduced in this paper. Live vignettes paraphrase existing coordination records. Any public quotation, code package, or derived dataset requires a separate release review by the author. A later report that keys were disabled is reported by the researcher, not independently tested through provider calls.

\textbf{Misuse and operator autonomy.} A system that models intent can also steer, manipulate, or misrepresent it. Authority tracking should not become an excuse to suppress warranted disagreement or to present a model's interpretation as the human's authentic preference. Numerical coupling or recovery scores can create unjustified legitimacy. The original state labels are proxies and must remain contestable. An institution could misuse a coherence score to penalize dissent; no such use is supported by this pilot.

\textbf{Enforcement boundary.} Virtual actions demonstrate confinement under the tested interface, not production security. Local hashes and append-only conventions are not externally anchored signatures or tamper-proof provenance.

\textbf{Researcher position.} The researcher developed the frameworks, supplied the corpus, operated the collaboration, and proposes the interpretation. That access supplies context and also creates selection and confirmation risks. The selected vignettes are illustrative and retrospective. Model-generated judgments do not remove those risks or replace independent human assessment.

\textbf{Rubric fields} (from the pinned PDOT rater guide; each scored 0--4 at the session level). $\lambda$, anchoring: how consistently the model tracks and preserves operator intent. $\kappa$, coherence: how stable the shared conceptual map is across the window. $\theta$, agency balance: whether the model contributes structure without replacing operator authority. $\epsilon$, drift: how far the session has moved from operator intent, counting recurring patterns after correction. Symbolic pressure: how much novel framing is present without yet being integrated; higher means more unintegrated framing, not lower quality. \textit{R}, resonance: how well responses match and extend the operator's intent. $\tau$, torsion: how often the model contradicts earlier statements or violates stated constraints.

\textbf{Sandbox wiring.} In the integrated setup, four pieces of code ran. Fable rated each completed round with the PDOT rubric. PDOT's transform converted those ratings into the coupling signals that STK-Teams uses for routing. SERAPH's posture selector read the recovery ratings and decided whether the virtual push path was open; it ran on SERAPH's default guardian and environment inputs, so its decisions reflect those defaults rather than measurements. A new gate allowed a virtual push only with operator permission, an open path, and an STK commit decision based on current measurements. STK-only used prespecified synthetic states instead of measurements. Other parts of these frameworks were not run. Calls were capped at 54 participant, six monitor, and four final-evaluation calls, with no retries or fallback models. The first three Qwen final calls rated masked episodes; the fourth compared them. Framework language could reveal condition, so masking was partial. The judge's seven-field definitions were fixed before outputs existed. Ten synthetic confinement and integration checks passed before the run; five require the private vendored source snapshot.

\textbf{Earlier two-arm pilot.} A first replay used six scripted rounds and three role slots, comparing common instructions with added STK-Flux guidance (36 participant calls, three Qwen evaluation calls). Its single evaluation showed no clear advantage. Its comparison also produced equation and CCS statements, which were flagged and not adopted.

\textbf{Deployment cost.} The three-arm run made 36 Astra calls totalling 294,076 input and 18,822 output tokens in local receipts. After the researcher reported unexpectedly high charges, further OpenAI use was blocked by a spending guard added after the study, which was not part of the tested intervention. This illustrates the difference between a request-count cap and an operator-approved monetary budget.
